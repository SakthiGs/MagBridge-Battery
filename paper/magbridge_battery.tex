\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Battery diagnostics is commonly based on terminal electrochemical
measurements, while magnetic sensing can provide complementary spatial
information about internal current distribution and magnetisation. Public
datasets combining magnetic measurements with degradation labels remain
scarce. We present MagBridge-Battery v1.0, a reproducible synthetic benchmark
built from two independent public sources: battery magnetometry provides
measured magnetic morphology, while PulseBat provides state-of-health,
state-of-charge, and pulse-derived features from retired
lithium-iron-phosphate cells. Because the sources contain different cells and
chemistries and are not experimentally paired, the generated signatures
should be interpreted as synthetic benchmark data rather than measured
magnetic ageing trajectories. The release contains 6,760 samples: 5,600
conditioned signatures, 600 controlled sensor-anomaly samples, and 560
low-voltage extrapolation-regime samples. Each sample contains six length-100
magnetic channels with provenance metadata. A cell-disjoint primary split
prevents conditioning-cell overlap and keeps synthetic anomalies with their
clean parents. Reference tasks assess recovery of the imposed state-of-health conditioning and its derived second-life class, plus anomaly detection and anomaly-subtype classification. The
dataset is intended for reproducible benchmarking and method development,
not safety-critical battery diagnosis.
\end{abstract}

\section*{Background \& Summary}

Data-driven battery health diagnostics rely primarily on
voltage, current, temperature, and impedance measurements.
Most public battery datasets reflect this measurement modality. Widely used resources such as the Stanford/MIT cycling dataset~\cite{severson2019} and the PulseBat retired-cell pulse-test dataset~\cite{tao2024pulsebat,tao2024pulsebatdata} provide electrochemical measurements across a range of degradation states, with state-of-health (SOH) information and, in some cases, longitudinal degradation trajectories. These resources have supported research on cycle-life prediction, capacity-fade estimation, state estimation, and second-life battery assessment. Recent experimental studies have also examined SOH estimation specifically
for second-life batteries using partial operating profiles and
electrochemical-impedance measurements~\cite{braco2022secondlife,
camboim2024secondlife}.

Recent advances in lithium-ion battery-state estimation
include hybrid deep-learning strategies for state-of-charge (SOC) estimation in large-scale energy-storage systems
\cite{wang2026hybrid}, history-aware recurrent models
for joint SOC and state-of-energy (SOE) estimation \cite{wang2025hlhgur}, and Bayesian-optimised bidirectional recurrent models combined with filtering for high-precision SOC estimation \cite{wang2025bilstm}. These studies demonstrate progress in temporal modelling and hybrid estimation using electrochemical and operational measurements. However, they do not address the separate scarcity of magnetic measurements paired with degradation labels. MagBridge-Battery addresses this complementary
data gap through a reproducible synthetic benchmark,
without claiming experimentally validated magnetic
ageing behaviour.

Magnetic sensing provides a complementary measurement modality because magnetic fields around a cell depend on internal current distributions and magnetic responses that are not directly observable from terminal measurements alone. Previous studies have demonstrated magnetic characterisation of lithium-ion cells using several sensing approaches, including magnetometry and magnetic imaging~\cite{ilott2018,romanenko2020,hu2020,ilott2017,nature2025magnetic,aachen2025}. Recent work has further demonstrated magnetic imaging of current density in
lithium-ion batteries~\cite{aachen2025} and operando magnetic microscopy of
battery dynamics~\cite{nature2025magnetic}. Applied initiatives such as the QuaLiProM project are also investigating atomic magnetometry combined with AI for SOH-oriented classification of aged lithium-ion cells and second-life
assessment~\cite{qualiprom}. These studies establish magnetic sensing as a potentially informative complementary modality, but they do not provide a large public dataset in which magnetic measurements are experimentally paired with degradation labels across many aged cells.

Public resources linking these two types of information therefore remain limited. The Open Science Framework (OSF) archive associated with Mohammadi, Ilott, and Jerschow~\cite{hu2020,mohammadi2020osf} provides experimentally measured magnetic scans of a lithium-ion cell at five operating voltages, but does not provide SOH-labelled ageing trajectories across multiple cells.  PulseBat~\cite{tao2024pulsebat,tao2024pulsebatdata}, in contrast, provides pulse-test measurements and degradation-related information from retired lithium-ion cells but contains no magnetic measurements. Importantly, these sources
represent different cells, experimental settings, and cell chemistries and are not experimentally paired.

MagBridge-Battery was constructed as a reproducible
cross-source synthetic benchmark using these complementary
resources. Measured OSF magnetic data provide the
magnetic-morphology scaffold. PulseBat supplies the
SOH and SOC conditioning variables, while its
pulse-voltage features are used upstream to derive
the operating voltage.

The bridge uses operating voltage, SOH, and SOC to
generate controlled synthetic variations of the
magnetic scaffold. No OSF magnetic measurement is
assumed to correspond to an individual PulseBat cell,
and the imposed SOH dependence is not an experimentally
established magnetic ageing mechanism. The resulting
signatures are therefore synthetic benchmark instances,
not measured magnetic degradation trajectories.

The release contains 6,760 samples: 5,600 conditioned magnetic signatures,
600 controlled sensor-anomaly samples, and 560 low-voltage
extrapolation-regime (Regime-B) samples. Each sample contains six length-100
magnetic channels with provenance metadata, and a cell-disjoint primary
benchmark split is provided. The dataset is intended for reproducible method
development, sensitivity analysis, and comparison of algorithms under a
documented synthetic construction. Reference tasks are provided for SOH regression and second-life classification, which assess recovery of the imposed SOH conditioning rather than detection of physical magnetic ageing, together with anomaly detection and anomaly-subtype classification. MagBridge-Battery is not intended as a
substitute for experimentally paired magnetic--electrochemical measurements
or for direct battery-health or safety-critical decision making. Instead,
the bridge assumptions, generation parameters, and provenance are exposed so
that methods developed on the benchmark can be evaluated against future
paired measurements when such data become available.

\section*{Methods}

\subsection*{Source data}

MagBridge-Battery draws on two independent public data sources that provide
complementary information and are not experimentally paired.

\textbf{Magnetic-morphology source.}
Magnetic morphology is derived from the OSF battery magnetometry archive of
Mohammadi, Ilott, and Jerschow~\cite{hu2020,mohammadi2020osf}
(OSF DOI: \texttt{10.17605/\allowbreak OSF.IO/\allowbreak CW8ZV}).
The archive contains magnetic-field measurements from a single lithium-ion
pouch cell at five operating-voltage anchors
$\{2.54, 2.81, 3.00, 3.10, 3.34\}$\,V, with 41 scan positions at each
voltage. Here, a scan position denotes one of the 41 spatial measurement
locations at which the magnetic field was sampled around the cell for a
given voltage anchor. The source experiment used a Powerstream PGEBNMU53040
pouch cell
with approximate dimensions $2.9\times3.9\times0.7$\,cm and a rated
capacity of 600\,mAh. The cell comprises a graphite anode and a
nickel--manganese--cobalt-oxide (NMC) cathode~\cite{hu2020}.
The magnetic field was measured using atomic magnetometers while the cell
was spatially scanned within a magnetically shielded susceptometry setup.
These measurements provide the experimentally observed signal-domain
morphology used to construct the bridge. The archive does not provide
SOH-labelled ageing trajectories across multiple cells; consequently,
no SOH value in MagBridge-Battery is interpreted as an experimentally measured
health state of the OSF cell. The OSF source is treated only as the experimentally measured
magnetic-morphology source; no chemistry match, ageing-history match, or
cell-level correspondence with the PulseBat conditioning population is
assumed. The OSF archive does not specify an explicit licence. Prof. Alexej
Jerschow, an author of the archive, confirmed by email that the data may be
reused with citation; this correspondence is available to the editors upon
request.

\noindent\textbf{Electrochemical-conditioning source.}
SOH, state-of-charge (SOC), and 21-dimensional pulse-voltage features are
drawn from PulseBat~\cite{tao2024pulsebat,tao2024pulsebatdata},
specifically version v3 of the TBSI-Lijing Battery Dataset archived on Zenodo
(DOI: \texttt{10.5281/\allowbreak zenodo.13360631}), a public pulse-test
dataset of retired lithium-ion cells with heterogeneous retirement conditions.
PulseBat uses constant-current--constant-voltage (CCCV) capacity-calibration
results as the SOH benchmarking capacity values, while pulse-response
measurements are used to derive the 21-dimensional feature representation.

MagBridge-Battery uses the calibrated SOH values supplied by PulseBat
directly as conditioning labels. In the upstream PulseBat processing
pipeline, SOH is defined as
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{SOH}=\frac{Q}{Q_n},
\end{equation}


where $Q$ is the calibrated discharge capacity obtained
from the capacity calibration test and $Q_n$ is the
nominal battery capacity. For the LFP subset used in
MagBridge-Battery, $Q_n=35$\,Ah.

PulseBat determines the calibrated capacity using
constant-current--constant-voltage (CCCV) charging
followed by constant-current discharge. The cell is
charged at 1C to the upper cut-off voltage and held
at that voltage until the current decreases to 0.05C.
It is then discharged at 1C to the lower cut-off
voltage. For the 35-Ah LFP cells, the upper and lower
cut-off voltages are 3.65\,V and 2.50\,V, respectively.

The resulting discharge capacity is treated as the
calibrated battery capacity. MagBridge-Battery does
not recompute SOH from the 21-dimensional pulse
features; it uses the upstream PulseBat SOH values
directly.

The pulse tests were conducted across SOC levels from 5\% to 50\% under a controlled ambient temperature of 25\,$^\circ$C. MagBridge-Battery uses exclusively the 35-Ah
lithium-iron-phosphate (LFP) subset of PulseBat. The PulseBat records supply the conditioning-cell identifiers, calibrated SOH values, SOC values, and pulse-voltage features used upstream to derive the operating voltage and retained in the released metadata; they do not contain magnetic measurements. The associated PulseBat project repository states that the raw data and processed features are available under the MIT licence.

The two sources are used with explicitly separated provenance: OSF provides the measured magnetic-morphology scaffold, whereas PulseBat provides the independent electrochemical conditioning population. No cell-level pairing, chemistry matching, ageing-history correspondence, or transfer of magnetic
ageing behaviour between the source populations is assumed. In particular, the OSF NMC morphology is used only as a measured signal-domain scaffold; the LFP conditioning variables are applied as synthetic benchmark controls, and no chemistry-specific NMC-to-LFP ageing relationship is assumed.
Consequently, the SOH-conditioned signatures are synthetic benchmark constructions rather than experimentally measured magnetic ageing trajectories.

\subsection*{Bridge overview}

The bridge is a deterministic function for fixed inputs,
configuration, and random seed:
$\mathcal{B}(v,\mathrm{SOC},\mathrm{SOH};\theta,s)
\rightarrow \mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times C}$,
where $v$ is the operating voltage, $\theta$ denotes
the bridge configuration, and $s$ is the per-sample
random seed. The generated magnetic signature has
length $T=100$ across $C=6$ channels.

For the released grounded dataset, the operating voltage $v$ is derived upstream from the mean of the 21 PulseBat pulse-voltage features and clipped to the grounded range $[3.06,3.34]$\,V. The pulse-feature vector is retained in the released provenance metadata but is not used directly by the bridge after the operating voltage has been determined. The OSF measurements and PulseBat-LFP records enter the bridge as independent sources with distinct roles: the former provides the magnetic-morphology scaffold, whereas the latter provides electrochemical conditioning variables. No sample-level pairing between the two sources is assumed.

In practical terms, generation proceeds in four steps. First, the requested voltage selects or interpolates an OSF-derived magnetic morphology. Second, PulseBat SOH is used to impose controlled synthetic SOH-dependent perturbations on that morphology, while SOC controls the low-frequency fluctuation term; the operating voltage is derived upstream from the PulseBat pulse-voltage features. Third, the perturbed representation is decoded back to the six-channel signal domain. Finally, sensor and SOC-dependent noise are added to produce the released synthetic signature.

The configuration $\theta$ collects all tunable generation
parameters and is fixed at bridge instantiation.
Generation is reproducible: a given
(request, $\theta$, seed) tuple always produces the
same output, and the configuration is recorded by
a hash in the released manifest.
Figure~\ref{fig:architecture} illustrates how
the OSF-derived morphology bank, regime-dependent
SOH conditioning, decoding operations, and noise
generation contribute to the construction of
grounded and Regime-B samples.

The resulting release is organised into three sample groups, as summarised in Fig.~\ref{fig:composition}. Of the 6,760 samples, 5,600 are grounded samples constructed within the source-supported operating domain using PulseBat-LFP conditioning variables, 600 are controlled synthetic anomaly
samples derived from clean parents, and 560 are low-voltage Regime-B samples for which SOH is undefined by design. The primary
\texttt{by\_cell\_primary} split is cell-disjoint and keeps each synthetic anomaly sample in the same partition as its clean parent.

\subsection*{Regime classifier}

The bridge operates in two voltage regimes, both recorded in the
\texttt{regime} metadata field. In the \emph{grounded} regime
(\texttt{regime}${=}$\texttt{grounded},
$v \in [3.06,3.34]$\,V), PulseBat electrochemical conditioning is
available. For each request, the base magnetic morphology is constructed
from the OSF voltage anchors that bracket the requested voltage according
to the interpolation procedure described below; the 3.06\,V value defines
the conditioning-regime boundary and is not itself an OSF measurement
anchor. The PulseBat-derived SOH and SOC variables are then applied as
synthetic conditioning variables.

In the \emph{extrapolation} regime
(\texttt{regime}${=}$\texttt{extrapolation}, also referred to as
Regime-B; $v \in [2.54,3.06)$\,V), PulseBat SOH conditioning is not
applied. Samples in this regime are generated from the OSF morphology
bank and are intended for low-voltage and out-of-distribution evaluation
rather than SOH regression. Voltages outside $[2.54,3.34]$\,V are
rejected. Every generated sample records its regime in the metadata.

Here, ``extrapolation'' refers to operation outside the PulseBat-conditioned
grounded regime; the Regime-B voltages themselves correspond to measured OSF
voltage anchors and therefore do not represent extrapolation beyond the OSF
magnetic measurement range.

Among the OSF voltage anchors, 3.10\,V and 3.34\,V lie within the grounded
regime and therefore provide direct anchor points for the distributional
validation reported in Technical Validation.

\subsection*{Morphology bank}

The OSF archive is canonicalised into per-anchor empirical statistics. At each of the five anchor voltages, the bridge extracts a mean trajectory $\boldsymbol{\mu}_v \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times C}$ and a per-timestep, per-channel standard deviation $\boldsymbol{\sigma}_v \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times C}$ over the 41 scan positions. For a generation request at voltage $v$ bracketed by anchors $v_l \leq v \leq v_h$, the bank forms a base sample by linear interpolation,
\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol{\mu}(v) = (1-\alpha)\,\boldsymbol{\mu}_{v_l} + \alpha\,\boldsymbol{\mu}_{v_h}, \qquad \alpha = \frac{v - v_l}{v_h - v_l},
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol{\sigma}(v) = \sqrt{(1-\alpha)\,\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{v_l}^2 + \alpha\,\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{v_h}^2},
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{X}_{\mathrm{base}} = \boldsymbol{\mu}(v) + \boldsymbol{\sigma}(v) \odot \boldsymbol{\epsilon}, \qquad \boldsymbol{\epsilon} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I).
\end{equation}
The measured OSF data therefore determine the anchor morphology, while intermediate-voltage and stochastic realisations are generated by the interpolation and sampling procedure above. At an exact anchor with no perturbation, the deterministic anchor-replica
configuration is defined to return the empirical mean trajectory; this
property is tested in Technical Validation.

\subsection*{SOH-conditioning module}

The SOH-conditioning module is a synthetic component of the bridge that applies controlled SOH-dependent perturbations to the base morphology through a fixed feature representation, termed MagBridge-Embed in the released pipeline. Here, SOH conditioning refers to controlled synthetic conditioning using the PulseBat SOH variable and does not imply that the imposed perturbation represents an experimentally established magnetic ageing mechanism.

MagBridge-Embed is a 171-dimensional reservoir-derived embedding used as a reproducible latent space for defining and decoding morphology perturbations. In this release, the embedding is produced by a fixed quantum reservoir encoder following the reservoir-computing principle of Fujii and Nakajima~\cite{fujii2017qrc}. The encoder uses 10 qubits arranged as 4 memory qubits and 6 processor qubits, two reservoir layers, and pooling over last, mean, and standard-deviation statistics. The reservoir circuit was implemented in PennyLane using the \texttt{default.qubit} simulator; the generation environment specified PennyLane version 0.40 or later. The reservoir parameters are not trained; only the downstream linear projections used inside the bridge are fitted. The encoder therefore acts as a deterministic feature transform within the
released generation pipeline rather than as a trained quantum model. It is retained in MagBridge-Battery because it is the representation used to generate the frozen released artifact; its use does not imply a claim of quantum advantage or performance optimality. The dependence of benchmark utility on this representation is examined using matched alternative representations in Technical Validation.

The module first computes the embedding $\mathbf{e}_{\mathrm{base}}$
of the base sample and projects it into a four-dimensional Linear
Discriminant Analysis (LDA) subspace fitted on real OSF samples with the
voltage anchors as classes,
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{z}_{\mathrm{base}} =
\big(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1/2}
(\mathbf{e}_{\mathrm{base}}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_e)
-\bar{\mathbf{x}}\big)\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{LDA}}.
\end{equation}

Here, $\mu_e$ and $\Sigma$ denote the fitted embedding centring and scaling
statistics used for standardisation, $\bar{x}$ is the LDA centring vector,
and $W_{\mathrm{LDA}}$ is the fitted four-dimensional LDA projection matrix.

The fitted unit direction $\hat{\mathbf{d}}$ is aligned with the ordered OSF voltage-anchor centroids in the four-dimensional LDA space, with the positive direction corresponding to increasing anchor voltage. A synthetic SOH-conditioning displacement is then applied along the fitted
direction $\hat{\mathbf{d}}$, with magnitude determined by
$\delta=\max(0,1-\mathrm{SOH})$,
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{z}_{\mathrm{pert}} =
\mathbf{z}_{\mathrm{base}} + m\hat{\mathbf{d}},
\qquad
m=-\gamma\delta.
\end{equation}
This displacement is a benchmark-generation rule rather than an
experimentally derived magnetic ageing trajectory. The fitted direction
$\hat{\mathbf{d}}$ is not interpreted as unique, causal, or physically
identified; alternative-direction ablations are evaluated in Technical
Validation.

The perturbed representation is decoded back to the time domain by a
cone-restricted $k$-nearest-neighbour softmin retrieval against the real
OSF samples,
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{X}_{\mathrm{decode}} =
\sum_{i\in\mathcal{N}_k}w_i\mathbf{X}^{\mathrm{OSF}}_i,
\qquad
w_i =
\frac{\exp(-d_i/\tau)}
{\sum_j\exp(-d_j/\tau)},
\end{equation}
with $k=8$ neighbours and a $75^\circ$ alignment cone. The decoded
morphology is then blended with the base sample according to
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{X}_{\mathrm{mod}} =
(1-\beta)\mathbf{X}_{\mathrm{base}}
+\beta\mathbf{X}_{\mathrm{decode}},
\qquad
\beta=\min(1,1.5\delta).
\end{equation}

In the released bridge implementation (v1.3), the
SOH-dependent latent displacement is applied opposite
to the fitted positive direction $\hat{\mathbf{d}}$,
whereas the cone filter evaluates candidate alignment
using the positive direction $\hat{\mathbf{d}}$.
Specifically, candidate displacements are measured
relative to $\mathbf{z}_{\mathrm{base}}$, and their
angular alignment is tested against
$\hat{\mathbf{d}}$, rather than against the signed
SOH-dependent displacement vector. Cone filtering is
bypassed for $\delta < 0.02$, and the unrestricted
candidate set is used when fewer than eight candidates
satisfy the angular criterion. This convention is
preserved in the frozen released dataset; a
sign-consistent alternative is evaluated separately
as a diagnostic in Technical Validation.

Two further SOH-scaled operations are applied after decoding. First, per-channel multiplicative amplitude jitter increases with \(\delta=\max(0,1-\mathrm{SOH})\). Second, zero-mean broadband Gaussian perturbations (referred to below as spectral broadening) are added with magnitude proportional to the same SOH difference. The released configuration sets the amplitude-strength and spectral-strength parameters to 0.30 and 0.05, respectively. These operations are part of the synthetic benchmark construction and are not calibrated representations of physical ageing. The PulseBat pulse-feature vector does not directly modulate these operations; in the released generation pipeline, it is used upstream to determine the operating voltage of each grounded request. The same configuration uses \(\gamma=800\), \(k=8\), \(\tau=50\), and a cone half-angle of \(75^\circ\); the full configuration is pinned by hash in the manifest. Sensitivity to these generation parameters is evaluated in Technical Validation.

\subsection*{Noise model}

Two additive noise sources are applied to the module output. Sensor noise is zero-mean Gaussian with per-channel standard deviation set to 5\% of the standard deviation of the SOH-conditioned signal over its 100 time points for the corresponding channel. An SOC-dependent fluctuation adds a low-frequency component scaled by $|\mathrm{SOC}-50|/50$, implemented as boxcar-smoothed Gaussian noise normalised and scaled to 4\% of the per-channel range. No additional SOH-dependent disorder term is applied, to avoid double-counting the spectral broadening already introduced by the SOH-conditioning module.

\subsection*{Synthetic sensor anomalies}

For the 600 anomaly samples, a clean parent sample is generated by the
pipeline above and one of four controlled perturbations is applied:
\texttt{sensor\_dropout}, \texttt{calibration\_drift},
\texttt{temporal\_warp}, or \texttt{periodic\_interference}
(150 samples each). These perturbations represent generic sensor and acquisition corruptions,
including missing measurements, calibration drift, timing distortion, and
periodic interference. Bias, drift, scaling, and dropout are established
sensor-fault modes~\cite{silva2012sensorfault}, while magnetic measurements
are also susceptible to external interference that can require explicit
compensation~\cite{janosek2019magnetic}. They are implemented as controlled
benchmark stress tests rather than as experimentally calibrated fault
distributions for a specific magnetometer or battery experiment. Their
severity values should therefore be interpreted as reproducible generation
settings, not as estimates of real-world fault prevalence or magnitude.
The parent identifier is retained in \texttt{parent\_sample\_id}, and
parent--child samples are kept within the same benchmark partition.

\subsection*{Reproducibility}

Generation is fully deterministic given the configuration $\theta$ and
per-sample seeds. The released manifest records the bridge version, a hash
of the bridge hyperparameters, the schema version, and per-sample generation
seeds, allowing the released generation configuration to be identified and
replayed. The bridge implementation, generation scripts, and unit tests are
openly available (Code Availability).

\section*{Data Records}

The MagBridge-Battery dataset is archived on
Zenodo~\cite{gunasekar2026magbridge_dataset} under a Creative Commons
Attribution 4.0 (CC-BY-4.0) licence. The release is distributed as a single
bundle containing five Parquet data shards of 1,352 rows each, a
metadata-only Parquet view, two benchmark-split files in JSON format, a
generation manifest, a minimal Python loader, and SHA-256 checksums for
every shipped file. The complete dataset contains 6,760 rows. Each row
corresponds to one synthetically generated magnetic-signature sample and
contains six length-100 magnetic channels, a shared length-100 normalised
time grid, conditioning and provenance metadata, and anomaly-related fields
where applicable.

\subsection*{Composition}

The 6,760 samples are partitioned into three provenance groups. The grounded
group contains 5,600 samples generated within the grounded voltage regime
using PulseBat-LFP SOH and SOC as conditioning variables, with the operating
voltage derived upstream from the PulseBat pulse-voltage features. These
samples provide the clean labelled subset for SOH regression and second-life
classification. The anomaly group contains 600 synthetic sensor-anomaly
samples derived from clean grounded parents, with 150 samples each for
\texttt{sensor\_dropout}, \texttt{calibration\_drift},
\texttt{temporal\_warp}, and \texttt{periodic\_interference}. Each anomaly
row stores the identifier of its clean parent. The extrapolation-regime (Regime-B) group contains 560 low-voltage samples generated at the Regime-B anchors
$\{2.54, 2.81, 3.00\}$\,V. These samples lie outside the PulseBat-LFP
conditioning range used in this release; therefore \texttt{soh},
\texttt{u\_features}, and \texttt{second\_life\_class} are set to missing
values by design. They are intended for low-voltage and
out-of-distribution evaluation rather than SOH regression.

\subsection*{Signal channels}

Each row contains six magnetic signal channels, each represented as a
length-100 sequence: \texttt{B\_s1Y}, \texttt{B\_s1Z},
\texttt{B\_s2Y}, \texttt{B\_s2Z}, \texttt{B\_s1C5}, and
\texttt{B\_s2C6}. The first four represent signed Y and Z field components
for two sensors. The final two are signed source channels retained from the
OSF archive. Although these channels are labelled as magnitude channels in
the source archive, their values are signed and may be negative; they are
therefore preserved as signed channels and should not be interpreted as
strict $\sqrt{Y^2+Z^2}$ magnitudes.

The \texttt{time\_norm} column contains 100 evenly spaced values in
$[0,1]$. This reference grid is identical for every sample and therefore
carries no sample-specific timing information. The
\texttt{temporal\_warp} anomaly perturbs signal values on this fixed grid
rather than introducing an irregular per-sample timebase.

\subsection*{Benchmark splits}

Two benchmark splits accompany the dataset. The primary split,
\texttt{by\_cell\_primary}, is intended for all headline results. It is
cell-disjoint and parent--child-leakage-free, with 4,507 training samples,
1,074 validation samples, and 1,179 test samples. The split has zero
PulseBat conditioning cells shared between partitions, zero
clean-parent/anomaly-child pairs divided across partitions, and zero
sample-identifier overlap.

The three Regime-B voltage-specific pseudo-cells are assigned one per
partition under the deterministic split procedure: the training,
validation, and test partitions contain 199, 178, and 183 Regime-B
samples, respectively, ensuring that all three partitions retain
Regime-B examples for the T3 benchmark.

A companion split, \texttt{by\_record\_optimistic\_baseline}, is provided
only as a leakage contrast. It contains quantified leakage, including
59 dataset cell identifiers appearing in more than one partition
(56 PulseBat conditioning-cell identifiers and three Regime-B
voltage-specific pseudo-cell identifiers) and 292 parent--child pairs
split across partitions. This companion split should not be used for
headline benchmarking; it is included to allow users to measure the
inflation caused by record-level splitting.

Each split is stored as a JSON object containing sample-identifier arrays
for training, validation, and testing, named \texttt{train\_samples},
\texttt{val\_samples}, and \texttt{test\_samples}, respectively. The split
files also include the corresponding conditioning-cell lists and split
metadata.

\subsection*{Second-life label definition}

The \texttt{second\_life\_class} field is a benchmark classification label
derived from the PulseBat SOH conditioning value using a fixed threshold of
SOH = 0.85. Samples with SOH $> 0.85$ are labelled \texttt{reuse}, whereas
samples with SOH $\leq 0.85$ are labelled \texttt{recondition}. This threshold
is used to provide a reproducible binary benchmark task and should not be
interpreted as an independently measured or universally applicable criterion
for second-life suitability.

\subsection*{Structural invariants}

Five structural invariants are tested automatically to verify release
consistency and implemented bridge behaviour. First, at each OSF anchor
voltage, the bridge's deterministic anchor-replica function (no perturbation,
no noise) reproduces the empirical mean trajectory to within machine
precision; the maximum absolute deviation observed is
$1.87 \times 10^{-12}$.

Second, for fixed (voltage, SOC, seed), increasing the imposed
SOH-conditioning magnitude produces monotonically increasing distance from
the SOH${=}1.0$ reference, confirming that the implemented conditioning
operation behaves as specified.

Third, a 0.01\,V change in voltage produces a smaller output change than a
0.10\,V change (observed L2 changes $\approx 261$ versus $\approx 1881$),
providing a numerical check of local voltage-response continuity.

Fourth, the regime classifier returns the expected regime at boundary
voltages and rejects out-of-range voltages. Fifth, anomaly flags are
consistent with the released schema: every extrapolation-regime and synthetic
anomaly sample carries \texttt{anomaly\_flag} true, whereas every grounded
clean sample carries it false.

In addition, the released artifacts were audited to confirm zero duplicate
sample identifiers, zero duplicate full-signal hashes, no NaN or infinity
entries in the signal channels, uniform length-100 arrays, and valid clean
parents for all 600 synthetic anomaly samples.

\subsection*{Distributional comparison at grounded anchors}

At the two grounded-regime OSF anchors (3.10\,V and 3.34\,V), 41 synthetic
samples per anchor were generated at SOH${=}1.0$ and compared with the
corresponding real OSF samples. Per-channel two-sample
Kolmogorov--Smirnov tests yielded $p>0.05$ for all six channels at both
anchors; these tests provide no evidence of a distributional difference
at the tested sample size, but should not be interpreted as establishing
distributional equivalence. Per-channel synthetic-to-real
standard-deviation ratios lie in $[0.98,1.02]$, indicating close agreement
in marginal amplitude variability.

The mean absolute difference between synthetic and real cross-channel
correlation matrices is $0.46$ at 3.10\,V and $0.35$ at 3.34\,V,
indicating that cross-channel dependence is less closely reproduced than
the marginal channel statistics. This residual is consistent with the
bridge's independent-channel noise assumption and is documented as a
known limitation in the Usage Notes.

\subsection*{Representation-dependence ablation}

To test whether benchmark utility depends on the released
quantum-reservoir encoder (QRec), we conducted a matched
regeneration experiment. Four alternatives were evaluated:
principal-component analysis (PCA), Gaussian random
projection, radial-basis-function (RBF) kernel PCA, and
a compact autoencoder. These cover linear, random,
nonlinear-kernel, and learned representations.

Each representation produced a 171-dimensional embedding
followed by the same four-dimensional LDA stage. All
variants used identical 5,600 generation requests,
conditioning records, per-sample seeds, bridge settings,
decoder, and post-processing. The fixed 39/8/9
train/validation/test cell-disjoint split across
56 conditioning cells and the evaluation procedure
were also identical; only the representation changed. Results are intended for within-ablation comparison
and are not reproductions of the reference values in
Table~\ref{tab:baseline}, which were evaluated directly
on the frozen DOI-archived release.

For each representation, the reported value is the mean performance of the best candidate downstream model under the matched evaluation protocol (T1: ridge regression, RBF support-vector regression, or random forest; T2: logistic regression, ridge classifier, linear support-vector classifier, or random forest). The hyperparameter-sensitivity and generation-seed analyses instead use fixed ridge-regression and logistic-regression readouts.

The RBF kernel-PCA control used a fixed kernel parameter of
$\gamma=1/600$, while the autoencoder used a fixed
$600\!-\!256\!-\!171\!-\!256\!-\!600$ reconstruction architecture.
Neither alternative was tuned against downstream T1 or T2 performance.

For SOH regression (T1), the QRec, PCA, random-projection, RBF
kernel-PCA, and autoencoder variants obtained mean $R^2$ values of
$0.6331$, $0.6717$, $0.6975$, $0.6686$, and $0.6414$, respectively.
For second-life classification (T2), balanced accuracy was $0.8246$,
$0.8261$, $0.8264$, $0.8296$, and $0.8195$, respectively. Thus, no
predictive advantage of the released QRec representation was observed
under this matched ablation. T2 performance remained similar across the
five representations, while PCA, random projection, and RBF kernel PCA
produced higher T1 performance than QRec; the autoencoder produced a
smaller T1 increase with slightly lower T2 performance.

These results indicate that the benchmark's decodable conditioning structure is not uniquely dependent on the quantum-reservoir representation. QRec is retained in MagBridge-Battery v1.0 because it is the representation used to generate the
frozen DOI-archived dataset and is therefore required for exact reproduction of that release, not because it provides superior predictive performance. The matched ablation shows that simpler classical and learned representations are viable alternatives and, in some settings, achieve higher downstream performance. Accordingly, no quantum-performance benefit or quantum-advantage
claim is made for QRec in this work.

\subsection*{Generation-seed uncertainty}

To quantify variability arising from stochastic generation, we independently regenerated the complete 5,600-sample grounded benchmark under five different master generation seeds. Each regeneration used the same 560 PulseBat-LFP conditioning records from 56 conditioning cells, ten variants per record, QRec/LDA representation, bridge hyperparameters, the same 39/8/9 train/validation/test split of those conditioning cells,
and downstream evaluation protocol. Only the random seed family used during synthetic generation was changed. For each regenerated dataset, T1 and T2 performance was averaged over the same five fixed model/subsampling seeds.

Across the five independently regenerated datasets, T1 SOH-regression
performance was $R^2 = 0.6252 \pm 0.0145$, with individual-run means ranging
from $0.6069$ to $0.6440$. T2 second-life balanced accuracy was
$0.8203 \pm 0.0157$, with individual-run means ranging from $0.7985$ to
$0.8376$. The reported standard deviations represent between-regeneration
variability rather than within-model fitting variability.

These results show that stochastic generation introduces measurable variation in absolute benchmark scores, while all five complete regenerations retain comparable predictive structure for both reference tasks. Generation-seed uncertainty should therefore be considered when interpreting small performance differences on the benchmark. This analysis assesses robustness of the synthetic construction and does not establish physical validity of the imposed SOH-conditioned magnetic morphology.

\subsection*{Relationship to real measurements}

The source datasets used here do not provide experimentally paired magnetic measurements and degradation labels for the same cells. Consequently, the SOH-conditioned synthetic signatures cannot be validated as measurements of real magnetic ageing trajectories. We therefore distinguish the aspects of
the bridge that are directly constrained by measured OSF data from those introduced synthetically by the generation procedure.

Three observations characterize this relationship. First, at each of the five OSF voltage anchors, the deterministic anchor-replica function reproduces the corresponding empirical OSF mean trajectory to within machine precision (maximum absolute deviation $1.87\times10^{-12}$). This verifies the implementation of the measured anchor morphology but does not imply
physical validation of subsequent SOH-conditioned transformations. Second, 
the grounded-regime base morphology is constructed by interpolation between
measured OSF anchors, and the perturbation decoder retrieves from the OSF
morphology bank. Thus, measured magnetic morphology directly constrains the
generation procedure, although subsequent conditioning, scaling, spectral
broadening, and noise remain synthetic operations. Third, at the two
grounded anchors, per-channel Kolmogorov--Smirnov tests yielded $p>0.05$
and synthetic-to-real standard-deviation ratios lie within $[0.98,1.02]$.
These results indicate close agreement in the tested marginal channel
statistics at the available sample size, but do not establish distributional
equivalence. Cross-channel correlation is reproduced less closely, as
quantified above.

These checks do not establish that the SOH-conditioned morphology changes
produced by the bridge correspond to those of a real cell at the same SOH.
The benchmark results should therefore be interpreted as internal reference
performance under the documented synthetic construction, rather than as
estimates of performance on experimentally measured magnetic ageing data.
The released provenance metadata and generation configuration are intended
to enable direct comparison with paired magnetic--electrochemical
measurements if such data become available.

\section*{Usage Notes}
\subsection*{Loading the data}

The released Parquet shards can be concatenated into a single table with any
Parquet-aware data library. A minimal loader script,
\texttt{load\_example.py}, is provided in the code repository; it loads all
shards, applies the \texttt{by\_cell\_primary} split, filters to the grounded
regime, and plots an example signature following the released benchmark
workflow. The signal channels and the \texttt{time\_norm} column are stored
as length-100 numeric arrays.

\subsection*{Recommended workflows}

State-of-health regression (T1) and second-life classification (T2) should
be performed on grounded-regime clean samples only; users should filter to
\texttt{regime == "grounded"} and exclude anomalies before fitting these
tasks, because extrapolation-regime samples carry \texttt{NaN} SOH by
design. Anomaly detection (T3) uses a mixture of held-out grounded clean
samples, synthetic anomalies, and extrapolation-regime samples. The three-class
T3 formulation is intentional: a binary comparison between extrapolation-regime
and grounded samples can largely reflect voltage-regime separation, whereas
T3 additionally requires synthetic anomalies to be distinguished from clean
grounded samples. Anomaly subtype classification (T4) should be performed on
the synthetic sensor-anomaly subset using the four defined perturbation
classes.

For reference benchmarking on the frozen, DOI-archived
MagBridge-Battery v1.0 dataset, users should adopt the
\texttt{by\_cell\_primary} split for all headline
results. This split prevents conditioning-cell overlap
and keeps synthetic anomaly samples in the same
partition as their clean parents.

The \texttt{by\_record\_optimistic\_baseline} split
is provided only as a leakage-prone comparison and
should not be used for headline benchmarking.

Controlled bridge-regeneration experiments reported
in Technical Validation use a separate, fixed
cell-disjoint split comprising 39 training, 8 validation,
and 9 test conditioning cells. Results from these
regeneration experiments are intended for within-study
comparisons and should not be directly compared with
the reference benchmark scores obtained using
\texttt{by\_cell\_primary}.


\subsection*{What the benchmark tasks measure}

T1 and T2 measure recovery of the SOH conditioning imposed by the synthetic bridge and the corresponding threshold-derived second-life label. Because the source datasets do not contain experimentally paired magnetic measurements and degradation trajectories for the same cells, these tasks do not measure detection of physical magnetic ageing, and their performance should not be interpreted as real-world SOH-estimation accuracy. 
T3 distinguishes clean grounded samples, synthetic sensor
anomalies, and Regime-B samples, while T4 classifies
the four synthetic sensor-anomaly subtypes. Neither task
establishes the ability to detect or classify real battery
or sensor faults.

\subsection*{Limitations users should be aware of}

The dataset is synthetic and should be interpreted according to the
assumptions of the bridge. The magnetic morphology bank is derived from
measurements of a single physical cell in the OSF archive, so inter-cell
variation in the underlying magnetic morphology is not represented directly.
Moreover, the magnetic-morphology and electrochemical-conditioning sources
are independent and are not chemistry matched: LFP in this release denotes the PulseBat conditioning population and should not be interpreted as the
chemistry of the OSF magnetic source or of experimentally measured
MagBridge signatures. Consequently, the imposed SOH-conditioned morphology
changes have not been validated as physical magnetic ageing trajectories. SOH is used here as a scalar conditioning variable and does not uniquely
encode degradation mechanism or ageing history; cells with similar SOH
values may therefore represent different underlying degradation pathways.

Several construction-specific limitations should also be considered. The
morphology bank is small, and the fitted latent perturbation direction is
not physically unique or mechanistically causal; alternative-direction and
representation ablations are reported in Technical Validation. The bridge
applies per-channel noise independently, leaving residual differences
between synthetic and real cross-channel correlation structure at the
grounded anchors. The \texttt{time\_norm} axis is a constant reference grid
and carries no per-sample timing information. The source channels
\texttt{B\_s1C5} and \texttt{B\_s2C6} are signed and should not be
interpreted as strict physical magnitudes. Synthetic sensor anomalies are
controlled benchmark perturbations rather than experimentally calibrated
fault distributions for a specific magnetometer.

Because the SOH-conditioned morphology is generated through a synthetic
conditioning procedure, models trained exclusively on MagBridge-Battery may
learn characteristics of the bridge itself, including its interpolation,
decoding, scaling, and noise structure, rather than physically meaningful
magnetic degradation mechanisms. Predictive performance on the released
benchmark should therefore be interpreted as evidence that a method can
recover the imposed conditioning structure under the documented bridge
assumptions, and not as evidence of real-world battery diagnostic accuracy.

The full-scale mechanism-knockout experiments further
demonstrate that recovery of the imposed SOH labels
depends predominantly on the synthetic morphology-decoding
and blending pathway. Disabling SOH-dependent blending
substantially reduced predictive performance, whereas
removing latent displacement alone preserved substantial
recoverability. Alternative perturbation directions
also retained predictive information. These results
show that benchmark performance does not uniquely
validate the fitted LDA direction or establish a
physically calibrated relationship between magnetic
morphology and battery degradation. Users should
therefore distinguish recovery of synthetically imposed
conditioning from prediction of experimentally
measured magnetic ageing.

The dataset is intended for reproducible benchmarking and method development,
not for safety-critical battery decisions or direct estimation of performance
on real magnetic ageing measurements. Every sample carries its generating
provenance, including operating voltage, SOC, SOH where applicable,
conditioning cell, seed, regime, anomaly fields, and bridge configuration
hash, supporting reproducible analysis and future comparison with paired
measurements.
